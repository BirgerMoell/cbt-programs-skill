Module 6 • Mindfulness and Present-Moment Awareness

Overview
  • Mindfulness is the practice of paying attention to the present moment with openness and curiosity. For people with GAD, this is particularly powerful because worry lives in the future—imagining what might happen. Mindfulness brings you back to the present, where most worries don't exist.
  • We explore how worry pulls you into the future, away from the present moment. When you're worrying, you're not actually experiencing what's happening right now—you're experiencing imagined scenarios. Mindfulness helps you return to what's actually happening.
  • The module introduces formal mindfulness practices: breath awareness, body scan, and mindful movement. These practices train your brain to stay present, creating new neural pathways that support present-moment awareness.
  • You'll learn informal mindfulness practices—bringing mindful awareness to everyday activities like eating, walking, or washing dishes. These practices help you integrate mindfulness into daily life.
  • We explore how mindfulness helps you observe worries without getting caught in them. Instead of being consumed by worry, you notice it, acknowledge it, and let it pass—like clouds in the sky.
  • Finally, we introduce the concept of 'worry as mental time travel'—how worry takes you out of the present moment into imagined futures. Mindfulness helps you recognize this and return to the present.
Key Takeaways
  • Mindfulness brings you back to the present moment, where most worries don't exist.
  • Worry lives in the future; mindfulness lives in the present.
  • Observing worries without getting caught in them reduces their power.
  • Regular mindfulness practice creates new neural pathways that support present-moment awareness.

Coming home to the present

Where are you right now? I don't mean physically—I mean mentally. If you're like most people with GAD, your mind is probably somewhere else. It's in the future, imagining what might happen. It's in the past, replaying what already happened. It's anywhere but here, in this moment.

Worry lives in the future. It imagines scenarios that haven't happened, problems that don't exist, threats that aren't real. When you're worrying, you're not experiencing what's actually happening—you're experiencing what might happen. And what might happen is almost always worse than what is happening.

Mindfulness brings you home to the present moment. It's the practice of paying attention to what's happening right now, with openness and curiosity. When you're mindful, you're here—not in an imagined future, not in a remembered past, but here, in this moment.

The present moment is usually okay. Right now, as you read this, are you safe? Are your basic needs met? Is something actually wrong, or are you just worried about what might be wrong? Most of the time, the present moment is fine. Worry takes you away from this fine moment into imagined difficult moments.

Exercise 1

Breath Awareness Meditation: Set a timer for 10 minutes. Sit comfortably, close your eyes, and focus on your breath. When your mind wanders (which it will), gently return your attention to your breath. Practice daily.

Worry as mental time travel

Think of worry as mental time travel. Your body is here, in the present, but your mind travels to the future, imagining scenarios that haven't happened. 'What if I get sick?' 'What if I lose my job?' 'What if something bad happens?'

This mental time travel feels productive. It feels like planning. But it's actually rumination—going over imagined scenarios repeatedly without taking action. And it keeps you away from the present moment, where life is actually happening.

Mindfulness helps you recognize this mental time travel. When you notice your mind has traveled to the future, you gently bring it back to the present. 'I notice I'm worrying about the future. Right now, I'm here, reading this. Let me return to this moment.'

This doesn't mean ignoring problems or avoiding planning. It means doing planning when it's useful, and staying present when it's not. Most of the time, it's not useful to be in the future—it's useful to be here, in the present.

Exercise 2

Body Scan Practice: Lie down comfortably. Slowly scan your body from head to toe, noticing sensations without judgment. Spend 15-20 minutes on this practice. Notice how it affects your awareness.

Observing worry without getting caught

One of the most powerful aspects of mindfulness is learning to observe your worries without getting caught in them. Instead of being consumed by worry, you notice it, acknowledge it, and let it pass—like clouds in the sky.

When a worry appears, you might think: 'I'm worrying about X.' This thought itself is a form of mindfulness—you're noticing the worry rather than being consumed by it. This creates space. Instead of being the worry, you're observing it.

From this space, you can choose how to respond. You can: Acknowledge the worry ('I notice I'm worrying'), Postpone it to worry time, Redirect your attention to the present moment, Or simply let it pass without engaging with it.

This takes practice. At first, worries will pull you in. That's normal. Gently redirect your attention each time. Over time, you'll get better at observing worries without getting caught in them.

Exercise 3

Mindful Eating: Choose one meal per day to eat mindfully. Notice the colors, textures, smells, and tastes. Eat slowly, savoring each bite. Notice how this differs from eating while worrying.

Formal and informal mindfulness

There are two types of mindfulness practice: Formal practice—scheduled time for mindfulness meditation (breath awareness, body scan, etc.), and Informal practice—bringing mindful awareness to everyday activities (eating, walking, washing dishes, etc.).

Both are important. Formal practice trains your brain to stay present, creating new neural pathways that support present-moment awareness. Informal practice helps you integrate mindfulness into daily life, so you can stay present even when you're not meditating.

Start with formal practice—even five minutes a day makes a difference. Then bring that awareness into your daily activities. Over time, mindfulness becomes a way of being, not just something you do.

  • Present-Moment Check-Ins: Set reminders on your phone to check in with the present moment. Ask: 'Where is my mind right now? Am I in the present or the future? What's actually happening right now?'
Homework
  • Practice breath awareness meditation daily for two weeks, starting with 10 minutes and gradually increasing if desired. Notice how it affects your ability to stay present.
  • Choose one daily activity (eating, walking, showering) to do mindfully. Bring full attention to the activity, noticing sensations, thoughts, and feelings without judgment.
  • Practice worry observation daily. When worries appear, observe them without getting caught. Notice how this creates space between you and your worries.
  • Complete present-moment check-ins three times per day. Notice where your mind is and gently return it to the present when it has wandered.
