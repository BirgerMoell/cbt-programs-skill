Breaking the cycle of avoidance

When you're burned out, avoidance feels like the only option. The thought of doing anything beyond the bare minimum feels overwhelming. You might find yourself: Canceling plans, Procrastinating on tasks, Spending hours scrolling mindlessly, Withdrawing from activities you used to enjoy, Saying 'no' to everything, even things that might help.

Avoidance provides temporary relief, but it reinforces burnout. It sends a message to your brain: 'I can't handle this.' It reduces opportunities for positive experiences. It creates a cycle where less activity leads to less energy, which leads to less activity.

Behavioral activation breaks this cycle. It's not about forcing yourself to do everything—it's about gradually re-engaging with activities that matter, that align with your values, and that generate positive feelings. Even small steps count.

The energy wheel: Understanding your resources

Imagine your energy as a wheel divided into four quadrants: Physical energy—your body's capacity for activity; Mental energy—your ability to focus, think, and problem-solve; Emotional energy—your capacity to feel, connect, and regulate emotions; Spiritual energy—your sense of purpose, meaning, and connection to something larger.

Burnout depletes all four quadrants. Recovery involves: Noticing which quadrants are most depleted, Identifying activities that restore each quadrant, Balancing activities that give energy with those that require energy, Protecting your energy by saying 'no' to non-essential demands.

You can't pour from an empty cup. This module helps you fill your cup—not all at once, but gradually, consistently, and compassionately.

Exercise 2

Energy Wheel Log: Record your energy levels in each quadrant (physical, mental, emotional, spiritual) three times daily for one week—morning, midday, and evening. Notice patterns and what activities affect each quadrant.

Micro-activities: Small steps, big changes

When you're exhausted, the thought of 'exercise' or 'socializing' or 'hobbies' can feel impossible. That's where micro-activities come in. These are tiny, manageable actions that: Take five minutes or less, Require minimal energy, Align with your values, Generate positive feelings, Build momentum for larger activities.

Examples include: A two-minute walk around the block, Listening to one favorite song, Texting a friend, Stretching for one minute, Writing three things you're grateful for, Looking at photos that bring joy, Drinking a glass of water mindfully.

These might seem too small to matter, but they do. Each micro-activity is a vote for recovery. Each one sends a message: 'I can do this. I'm taking care of myself. I'm moving forward.'

Desk-Stretch Routine: Perform gentle stretches at your desk three times daily. Focus on neck, shoulders, back, and wrists. Notice changes in tension, energy, and focus.

Sleep, nutrition, and movement: The foundation

Recovery from burnout requires addressing the basics. Your body needs: Adequate sleep—aim for 7-9 hours per night, with consistent sleep and wake times; Nutritious food—regular meals with protein, complex carbohydrates, and vegetables; Movement—even gentle movement helps regulate mood, energy, and stress hormones; Light exposure—natural light, especially in the morning, helps regulate circadian rhythms.

These aren't luxuries—they're necessities. When you're burned out, it's easy to let these basics slide. But they're the foundation everything else builds on. This module helps you prioritize them, even when it feels difficult.

Homework
  • Complete the Activity Hierarchy Worksheet and schedule your chosen micro-activities in your calendar. Commit to completing them, even if you don't feel like it.
  • Maintain the Energy Wheel Log for one week. At the end of the week, reflect on patterns: Which quadrants are most depleted? What activities restore energy? How can you protect and replenish your energy?
  • Keep a sleep and nutrition diary for seven consecutive days. Record sleep times, wake times, meals, and how you feel. Review patterns and identify one small change to improve sleep or nutrition.
