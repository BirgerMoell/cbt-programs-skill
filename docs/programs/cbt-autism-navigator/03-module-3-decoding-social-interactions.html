Module 3 • Decoding Social Interactions

Overview
  • Having established a foundation of self-regulation, we now turn our attention outward to one of the most complex and energy-intensive aspects of daily life: social interaction. For many autistic people, the social world can feel like a game where everyone else intuitively knows the rules.
  • Social interaction is not random—it is a system based on patterns. For a mind that excels at identifying patterns, this is a powerful reframe. Instead of trying to 'feel' your way through a conversation, you can learn to observe and analyze it.
  • Most casual conversations follow a predictable, four-part structure: Initiation & Greetings, Small Talk as Data Exchange, Exchanging Information, and Ending a Conversation. Understanding this architecture allows you to know where you are in an interaction and what is expected next.
  • Small talk is not pointless chatter—it's a low-stakes data-gathering process to establish connection and search for common ground. A simple formula is Observation + Question.
  • Your autistic strength in observation is your greatest asset. Become a 'social detective' by observing from a distance, watching TV with a purpose, and formulating hypotheses about social rules.
  • Social anxiety can be managed with CBT by challenging catastrophic thoughts. Ask: What is the evidence? What's a different way of looking at this? What can I do to prepare?
Key Takeaways
  • Social interaction is a system of patterns, not a mystery. Your pattern-recognition skills are your greatest asset.
  • Conversations follow a predictable architecture: Initiation, Small Talk, Information Exchange, and Ending.
  • Small talk is data exchange—use the Observation + Question formula.
  • Being a social detective turns passive observation into active, structured learning.

Exercises
  • Your Exit Line: Choose one exit line from the examples that feels comfortable for you. Write it down and commit it to memory.
  • Observation Log: Watch a 5–10 minute scene of a television show where people are having a casual conversation. Identify the parts: initiation, small talk topic, transition to deeper topic, and how it ended.
  • Develop a Small Talk Script: Choose one specific, common situation where you often need to make small talk. Create a simple, 3-step script: The Opener, The Follow-up, and The Gentle Exit.
Homework
  • Observation Log: Watch a TV scene and document the conversational architecture, turn-taking patterns, and one 'unwritten rule' you observed.
  • Small Talk Script: Write down your 3-step script for a common situation and rehearse it.
  • Anxiety Log: If you experience social anxiety this week, apply the S-T-E-S-B model. Document the cycle and challenge the automatic thought with one realistic alternative perspective.
