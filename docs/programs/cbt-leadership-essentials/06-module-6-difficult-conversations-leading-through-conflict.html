Module 6 • Difficult Conversations: Leading Through Conflict

Overview
  • Difficult conversations are inevitable in leadership: giving feedback, addressing performance issues, handling conflicts, delivering bad news. How you handle them defines your leadership.
  • Most leaders avoid difficult conversations because they're uncomfortable. But avoiding them makes problems worse. This module teaches you to have them well.
  • You'll learn frameworks for difficult conversations, techniques for de-escalation, and skills for creating positive outcomes even from challenging situations.
Key Takeaways
  • Difficult conversations are necessary: avoiding them makes problems worse.
  • Preparation enables confidence: knowing your framework reduces anxiety.
  • Focus on behavior, not character: address actions, not personality.
  • Seek to understand: most conflicts come from misunderstanding, not malice.

Why difficult conversations are essential

Difficult conversations are some of the most important conversations you'll have as a leader. They're where problems get solved, relationships get repaired, and growth happens.

But most leaders avoid them. They're uncomfortable. They're risky. They might go badly. So we delay, we hope problems resolve themselves, we let issues fester.

Avoiding difficult conversations creates: Unresolved conflicts, Deteriorating relationships, Performance issues that persist, and Missed opportunities for growth.

Learning to have difficult conversations well is a superpower. It sets you apart, builds trust, and drives results.

The framework: preparation, delivery, follow-up

Every difficult conversation has three phases: Preparation (knowing what to say and how), Delivery (having the conversation), and Follow-up (ensuring resolution and learning).

Preparation is crucial: Clarify your purpose (what do you want to achieve?), Gather facts (what actually happened?), Consider their perspective (how might they see this?), Plan your approach (what will you say?), and Choose timing and setting (when and where?).

Delivery requires: Starting with context (why this conversation matters), Stating facts clearly (what happened, without judgment), Expressing impact (how it affected you or work), Listening to their perspective (understanding their side), and Finding solutions (working together on next steps).

Follow-up ensures: Understanding what was agreed, Taking action on commitments, Checking in on progress, and Learning for next time.

Giving difficult feedback

Giving difficult feedback is one of the hardest leadership skills. The SBI framework helps: Situation (when and where), Behavior (what they did), Impact (how it affected you, the team, or the work).

Example: 'In yesterday's team meeting (Situation), you interrupted Sarah three times (Behavior). This made it hard for her to finish her thoughts and seemed dismissive to the team (Impact).'

After stating SBI, invite their perspective: 'I'd like to understand your perspective. What was happening for you?' Then work together on solutions.

Focus on behavior, not character. 'You were late' is better than 'You're unreliable.' Behavior can change. Character feels fixed.

Handling performance issues

Performance conversations are among the most difficult, but they're essential for growth and accountability.

When addressing performance: Be specific (concrete examples, not generalizations), Be timely (address issues when they arise, not months later), Be balanced (acknowledge strengths while addressing gaps), Be collaborative (work together on improvement plans), and Be clear about consequences (what happens if improvement doesn't occur).

Frame performance conversations as growth opportunities, not punishments. The goal is improvement, not blame.

Document performance conversations: follow up in writing, create improvement plans, and track progress. Documentation protects everyone and ensures clarity.

De-escalating conflict

When conversations get heated, de-escalation is essential. Techniques include: Stay calm (your calm can calm them), Listen actively (show you're trying to understand), Use 'I' statements (focus on your experience, not their character), Avoid blame (focus on solutions, not fault), and Take breaks if needed (some conflicts need time to cool down).

Most conflicts come from misunderstanding, not malice. When you seek to understand, you often find common ground.

If a conversation isn't productive, pause. 'I think we're both getting frustrated. Can we take a break and come back to this in 30 minutes?'
