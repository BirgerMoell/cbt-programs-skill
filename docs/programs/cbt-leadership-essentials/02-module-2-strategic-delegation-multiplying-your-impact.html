Module 2 • Strategic Delegation: Multiplying Your Impact

Overview
  • Delegation is the superpower of leadership. When done well, it multiplies your impact, develops your team, and frees you to focus on what only you can do.
  • Most new leaders struggle with delegation: they either delegate too little (micromanaging) or too much (dumping). This module teaches you to delegate strategically.
  • You'll learn what to delegate, how to delegate effectively, and how to follow up without micromanaging.
Key Takeaways
  • Delegation is not dumping: it's a strategic decision about who does what and why.
  • What you delegate matters more than how much: delegate the right things to the right people.
  • Clear expectations prevent problems: ambiguity is the enemy of effective delegation.
  • Follow-up is essential, but it's not micromanaging: check progress, provide support, but don't take over.

Why delegation is essential

As an individual contributor, you had 24 hours in a day. As a leader, you still have 24 hours, but now you're responsible for much more. The only way to scale your impact is through delegation.

Great leaders don't do more—they enable others to do more. Delegation is how you multiply your impact, develop your team, and focus on what only you can do.

But delegation is hard. It requires trust, letting go of control, and accepting that others might do things differently than you would. It's worth it.

What to delegate: the delegation framework

Not everything should be delegated. Some things only you can do: strategic decisions, high-stakes conversations, developing other leaders, and work that requires your specific expertise.

Everything else can potentially be delegated. Use this framework: Delegate tasks that develop others (growth opportunities), Delegate routine work (frees your time), Delegate work others can do better (leverage expertise), and Delegate work that's not your highest value (focus on what matters).

Don't delegate: work that's confidential or sensitive, work that requires your specific expertise, strategic decisions, and work that's a growth opportunity for you.

When in doubt, ask: 'Is this the best use of my time?' If not, consider delegating.

How to delegate effectively

Effective delegation has five steps: Choose the right person (match task to capability and development needs), Set clear expectations (what, why, when, how, success criteria), Provide context and resources (why this matters, what they need), Establish checkpoints (when and how you'll check in), and Define authority (what decisions can they make?).

The most common delegation mistake is unclear expectations. Be specific: 'I need a report on Q3 sales by Friday' is better than 'Can you look into the sales numbers?'

Another common mistake is not providing context. People do better work when they understand why it matters. Share the bigger picture.

Finally, define authority clearly. Can they make decisions? Spend money? Commit the team? Ambiguity creates problems.

Following up without micromanaging

The line between follow-up and micromanaging is thin but important. Follow-up is: checking progress at agreed checkpoints, providing support when needed, and course-correcting when off track.

Micromanaging is: checking constantly, taking over when things get hard, and not trusting others to execute.

The key is to agree on checkpoints upfront. 'Let's check in on Tuesday to see how it's going' is follow-up. 'Can you send me updates every hour?' is micromanaging.

Trust is built through successful delegation. Start with smaller, lower-risk tasks. As trust builds, delegate more significant work.
