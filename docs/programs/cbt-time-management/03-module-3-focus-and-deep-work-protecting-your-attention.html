Module 3 • Focus and Deep Work: Protecting Your Attention

Overview
  • In a world of constant distractions, the ability to focus deeply is a superpower. This module teaches you to create conditions for deep work, protect your attention, and produce your best work consistently.
  • Deep work—focused, uninterrupted work on cognitively demanding tasks—is where real value is created. But it requires intention, boundaries, and systems.
  • You'll learn to eliminate distractions, create focus-friendly environments, build deep work habits, and protect the attention that drives exceptional results.
Key Takeaways
  • Deep work creates disproportionate value: protect time for it daily.
  • Distractions are expensive: each interruption costs focus and time.
  • Environment matters: create spaces that support focus.
  • Focus is a skill: practice it like any other skill.

Why focus is your competitive advantage

In an attention economy, the ability to focus deeply is increasingly rare—and increasingly valuable. While others are distracted, you can produce work that stands out.

Deep work isn't just about getting things done—it's about doing your best work. Shallow work (email, meetings, administrative tasks) is necessary, but deep work (strategic thinking, creative problem-solving, complex analysis) is where you create unique value.

This module helps you create the conditions for deep work and build the habits that protect your focus.

Exercise 1

Deep vs. shallow audit: Track your work for 3 days. Categorize each activity as deep or shallow. What's your current split? What would you like it to be?

Understanding deep work vs. shallow work

Deep work is: Cognitively demanding (requires focus and concentration), High-value (creates significant impact), Hard to replicate (requires your specific skills), and Best done in long, uninterrupted blocks.

Shallow work is: Low cognitive demand (can be done while distracted), Lower value (necessary but not strategic), Easy to replicate (others could do it), and Can be done in short bursts.

The goal isn't to eliminate shallow work—it's to minimize it and protect time for deep work. Most people spend too much time on shallow work and too little on deep work.

Assess your current split: How much time do you spend in deep work? Shallow work? What would an ideal split look like? How can you shift the balance?

Exercise 2

Distraction inventory: List your top 5 distractions. For each, create one strategy to eliminate or minimize it. Implement this week.

Eliminating distractions: creating focus conditions

Distractions are the enemy of focus. Every notification, every interruption, every context switch costs you focus and time. Eliminating distractions is essential for deep work.

Common distractions: Notifications (email, Slack, phone, apps), Open tabs and apps (visual clutter and temptation), Interruptions (colleagues, meetings, calls), Your own habits (checking email constantly, multitasking), and Internal distractions (worry, planning, mental chatter).

Elimination strategies: Turn off notifications (all of them, during deep work), Close unnecessary tabs and apps (minimal workspace), Set boundaries (do not disturb, closed door, headphones), Batch shallow work (handle email and messages in dedicated blocks), and Practice single-tasking (one thing at a time).

Your phone is often the biggest distraction. During deep work, put it in another room, turn it off, or use focus modes that block apps.

Create a distraction-free environment: physical space (quiet, organized), digital space (clean desktop, closed tabs), and mental space (clear mind, focused intention).

Exercise 3

Time-blocking practice: Create a time-blocked schedule for next week. Include deep work blocks, shallow work blocks, and breaks. Protect the deep work blocks.

Time-blocking for deep work

Time-blocking is scheduling specific blocks of time for specific work. It's essential for protecting deep work time.

How to time-block: Identify your deep work tasks (what requires focus?), Schedule them during your peak energy times (when are you most focused?), Block 2–4 hour chunks (deep work needs time), Protect these blocks (treat them like important meetings), and Batch similar work (group related tasks together).

Your calendar should show: Deep work blocks (protected, non-negotiable), Shallow work blocks (email, admin, meetings), Buffer time (for the unexpected), and Breaks (essential for sustained focus).

Start with 2–3 deep work blocks per week. Build the habit. Increase as you see the value.

Communicate your blocks: let your team know when you're in deep work mode. Set expectations about availability.

Building deep work habits

Deep work is a habit, not an event. Building the habit requires: Ritual (consistent routine that signals focus time), Environment (space that supports focus), Rules (boundaries that protect focus), and Practice (regular repetition that strengthens the habit).

Create a deep work ritual: Same time each day (builds habit), Same place (signals focus), Same preparation (coffee, music, setup), and Same intention (what will you focus on?).

Start small: 30 minutes of deep work daily. Build to 2–4 hour blocks. Consistency matters more than duration initially.

Track your deep work: How many hours this week? What did you accomplish? How did it feel? Use tracking to build awareness and motivation.

Deep work gets easier with practice. Your ability to focus is like a muscle—it strengthens with use.

Managing energy for sustained focus

Focus requires energy. You can't do deep work when you're exhausted. Managing your energy is essential for sustained focus.

Energy management for focus: Work during peak energy times (schedule deep work when you're naturally focused), Take breaks (pomodoros, walking, rest), Fuel your body (eat well, stay hydrated, move), and Protect sleep (rest is essential for focus).

The Pomodoro Technique: 25 minutes of focused work, 5-minute break, repeat. After 4 pomodoros, take a longer break. This rhythm supports sustained focus.

Breaks aren't wasted time—they're focus maintenance. Step away, move, rest your mind. You'll return more focused.

When you're low energy, do shallow work. Save deep work for when you have the energy to do it well.
