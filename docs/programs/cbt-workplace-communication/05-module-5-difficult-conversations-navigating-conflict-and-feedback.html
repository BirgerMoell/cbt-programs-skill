Why difficult conversations are essential

Difficult conversations are some of the most important conversations you'll have at work. They're where problems get solved, relationships get repaired, and growth happens. But most people avoid them, making problems worse.

Avoiding difficult conversations creates: unresolved conflicts, deteriorating relationships, performance issues that persist, and missed opportunities for growth.

Learning to have difficult conversations well is a superpower. It sets you apart, builds trust, and drives results. This module gives you the framework and skills to do it.

The framework: preparation, delivery, and follow-up

Every difficult conversation has three phases: Preparation (knowing what to say and how), Delivery (having the conversation), and Follow-up (ensuring resolution and learning).

Preparation is crucial: Clarify your purpose (what do you want to achieve?), Gather facts (what actually happened?), Consider their perspective (how might they see this?), Plan your approach (what will you say?), and Choose timing and setting (when and where?).

Delivery requires: Starting with context (why this conversation matters), Stating facts clearly (what happened, without judgment), Expressing impact (how it affected you or work), Listening to their perspective (understanding their side), and Finding solutions (working together on next steps).

Follow-up ensures: Understanding what was agreed, Taking action on commitments, Checking in on progress, and Learning for next time.

Giving constructive feedback: the SBI framework

Giving feedback is a skill. The SBI framework (Situation, Behavior, Impact) helps you give feedback that's specific, actionable, and well-received.

Situation: When and where did this happen? Be specific about context.

Behavior: What did they do? Focus on observable actions, not assumptions about intent or character.

Impact: How did their behavior affect you, the team, or the work? Be clear about consequences.

Example: 'In yesterday's team meeting (Situation), you interrupted Sarah three times while she was presenting (Behavior). This made it hard for her to finish her thoughts and seemed dismissive to the team (Impact).'

After stating SBI, invite their perspective: 'I'd like to understand your perspective. What was happening for you?' Then work together on solutions.

Receiving feedback: turning criticism into growth

Receiving feedback is just as important as giving it. How you receive feedback determines whether you grow from it or become defensive.

Effective feedback reception: Listen fully (don't interrupt or defend), Ask clarifying questions ('Can you help me understand...?'), Acknowledge what you heard ('So what I'm hearing is...'), Thank them ('I appreciate you sharing this'), and Reflect and act ('Let me think about this and get back to you').

Don't get defensive: even if feedback feels unfair, listen first. You can process and respond later. Defensiveness shuts down communication.

Separate feedback from identity: feedback is about behavior, not who you are. You can change behavior. You're not defined by one piece of feedback.

Ask for specifics: if feedback is vague ('You need to be more professional'), ask for examples. Specific feedback is actionable.

Handling conflicts: de-escalation and resolution

Conflicts happen. How you handle them determines whether they destroy relationships or strengthen them.

Conflict resolution steps: Acknowledge the conflict (don't pretend it doesn't exist), Understand perspectives (yours and theirs), Find common ground (what do you both want?), Focus on interests, not positions (why do you want what you want?), and Work together on solutions (collaborative, not competitive).

De-escalation techniques: Stay calm (your calm can calm them), Listen actively (show you're trying to understand), Use 'I' statements (focus on your experience, not their character), Avoid blame (focus on solutions, not fault), and Take breaks if needed (some conflicts need time to cool down).

Most conflicts come from misunderstanding, not malice. When you seek to understand, you often find common ground.

Performance conversations: addressing issues directly

Performance conversations are among the most difficult, but they're essential for growth and accountability.

When addressing performance: Be specific (concrete examples, not generalizations), Be timely (address issues when they arise, not months later), Be balanced (acknowledge strengths while addressing gaps), Be collaborative (work together on improvement plans), and Be clear about consequences (what happens if improvement doesn't occur).

Frame performance conversations as growth opportunities, not punishments. The goal is improvement, not blame.

Document performance conversations: follow up in writing, create improvement plans, and track progress. Documentation protects everyone and ensures clarity.
