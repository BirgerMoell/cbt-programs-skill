Stress and the heart

When you're stressed, your body releases adrenaline and cortisol. Your heart rate increases. Your blood vessels constrict. Your blood pressure rises. This is the 'fight or flight' response—useful in emergencies, harmful when chronic.

Many people live in a state of chronic stress. Work deadlines, family responsibilities, financial worries, health concerns—the list goes on. Your body stays in 'alert mode,' keeping blood pressure elevated.

But you can dial down the stress response. Through breathing, relaxation, and mindset shifts, you can signal to your body that it's safe to relax. Your heart can slow. Your blood vessels can widen. Your blood pressure can drop.

The relaxation response

Just as stress activates the 'fight or flight' system, relaxation activates the 'rest and digest' system. This is controlled by the parasympathetic nervous system—the brake pedal for your stress response.

You can activate this system intentionally. Deep, slow breathing. Progressive muscle relaxation. Meditation. These techniques aren't just 'nice to have'—they're medicine for your heart.

We're going to practice these techniques regularly. Not just when you're stressed, but daily. Like medication, they work best when taken consistently.

Changing your stress response

Some stress is unavoidable. But your response to stress is changeable. You can't control what happens, but you can control how you react.

This starts with awareness. Notice when stress is building. Notice the physical signs (racing heart, tension, shallow breathing). Then intervene early, before stress escalates.

We're going to build a toolkit of responses. Breathing exercises for immediate relief. Relaxation techniques for deeper calm. Cognitive strategies for reframing stressors. You'll have options.
