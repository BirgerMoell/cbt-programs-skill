Module 4 • Dietary Modifications for Heart Health

Overview
  • This module addresses the critical role of diet in blood pressure management, focusing on the DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) principles.
  • We explore 'Sodium and Blood Pressure'—understanding how excess salt affects cardiovascular health and learning to reduce sodium intake.
  • You will learn about 'Heart-Healthy Nutrients'—potassium, magnesium, calcium, and fiber—and how to incorporate them into your diet.
  • The concept of 'Portion Awareness' is introduced. You don't need to diet—just become aware of portions and make gradual adjustments.
  • We address 'Practical Meal Planning'—making heart-healthy eating realistic and sustainable, not restrictive.
  • You will create a 'Food & Blood Pressure Log'—tracking how different foods and meals affect your readings.
  • Finally, we practice 'Sustainable Changes'—making small, gradual dietary modifications that stick long-term.
Key Takeaways
  • Diet significantly impacts blood pressure, but extreme restrictions are unsustainable.
  • Reducing sodium is one of the most effective dietary changes for hypertension.
  • Small, gradual changes are more sustainable than major overhauls.
  • Heart-healthy eating can be delicious and satisfying.

Food as medicine

What you eat directly affects your blood pressure. Sodium raises it. Potassium, magnesium, and fiber help lower it. Your diet is medicine—or poison—depending on what you choose.

But this isn't about deprivation. It's about addition and substitution. More vegetables, fruits, whole grains. Less processed food, less salt. You're not losing foods—you're gaining health.

We're going to make changes gradually. One meal at a time. One swap at a time. Building new habits that feel natural, not forced.

Exercise 1

The Sodium Audit: Track your sodium intake for 3 days. Read labels. Add it up. Compare to the recommended 2,300mg (ideally 1,500mg) per day. Where is most sodium coming from?

The sodium story

Most people consume far more sodium than they need. Processed foods, restaurant meals, even 'healthy' foods can be loaded with salt. Your body retains water to balance the sodium, increasing blood volume and pressure.

Reducing sodium doesn't mean tasteless food. Herbs, spices, citrus, and vinegar can add flavor without salt. You'll also find that your taste buds adapt—foods you once found bland will start tasting rich.

We're going to become sodium detectives. Reading labels. Asking questions. Making swaps. Every reduction helps.

Exercise 2

The Food-BP Connection: For one week, log meals and take blood pressure readings 1-2 hours after eating. Notice patterns: Do certain foods affect your readings?

Building a heart-healthy plate

A heart-healthy plate is colorful and varied. Vegetables and fruits (rich in potassium). Whole grains (fiber). Lean proteins. Healthy fats. This is the DASH diet approach—proven to lower blood pressure.

You don't need to follow a strict meal plan. Just aim for balance. More plants. Less processed. More home cooking. Less restaurant food. Simple principles, flexible application.

We're going to make this practical. Quick meal ideas. Easy swaps. Strategies for eating out. Real life, real food, real results.

Homework
  • Reduce sodium intake by 25% this week. Use the Sodium Audit to identify your biggest sources. Make targeted reductions.
  • Add one serving of potassium-rich food daily (banana, spinach, sweet potato, etc.). Notice how this feels.
  • Plan and prepare one heart-healthy meal this week. Make it something you'll actually enjoy and want to repeat.
