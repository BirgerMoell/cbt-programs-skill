Module 3 • Challenging Avoidant Behaviors: Opening to Connection

Overview
  • Avoidant attachment involves specific behaviors: withdrawal, emotional suppression, self-reliance, and distancing. This module helps you identify these behaviors and challenge them.
  • You'll learn to recognize when you're withdrawing, why you're doing it, and how to choose differently. You'll practice staying present in relationships even when it's uncomfortable.
  • Changing behaviors changes relationships. When you can stay present instead of withdrawing, you can build deeper connections.
Key Takeaways
  • Avoidant behaviors are patterns, not truths: you can choose differently.
  • Withdrawal creates distance: staying present creates connection.
  • Discomfort is temporary: the benefits of connection are lasting.
  • Small steps matter: you don't have to change everything at once.

Understanding avoidant behaviors

Avoidant attachment involves specific behaviors: Withdrawal (pulling away when relationships get close), Emotional suppression (dismissing or minimizing feelings), Self-reliance (handling everything alone, refusing help), Distancing (creating physical or emotional space), Intellectualization (thinking instead of feeling), and Deactivation (shutting down when intimacy increases).

These behaviors aren't random—they're protective strategies. They keep you safe from the vulnerability of depending on others. But they also prevent deep connection.

The goal isn't to eliminate these behaviors completely—it's to recognize them, understand them, and choose differently when appropriate.

Recognizing withdrawal patterns

Withdrawal is a core avoidant behavior. You might withdraw physically (spending less time together), emotionally (shutting down feelings), or mentally (checking out of conversations).

Common withdrawal triggers: Partner expresses strong emotions, Partner needs support, Relationship gets too close, Conflict or disagreement, Partner wants more intimacy, or Feeling overwhelmed by connection.

Notice your withdrawal patterns: When do you withdraw? What triggers it? How does it feel? What happens in your body? Understanding your patterns is the first step to changing them.

Exercise 2

Withdrawal awareness: This week, notice when you want to withdraw. Pause. Ask yourself: 'What am I feeling? What am I afraid of?'

Challenging withdrawal

When you notice the urge to withdraw, pause. Ask yourself: 'What am I feeling right now? What am I afraid of? What would happen if I stayed present?'

Practice staying present: Take a breath. Notice the discomfort. Stay anyway. You don't have to do anything—just be present. The discomfort will pass.

Start small: Stay present for 5 more minutes. Share one feeling. Ask one question. Small steps build capacity for connection.

Withdrawal is a habit. Like any habit, it can be changed. Each time you choose to stay present instead of withdrawing, you're building new patterns.

Exercise 3

Staying present practice: Choose one situation this week where you'd normally withdraw. Stay present instead. Notice what happens.

Challenging self-reliance

Self-reliance is valuable, but when it becomes a barrier to connection, it's a problem. If you never ask for help, never depend on others, you miss the opportunity for deeper relationships.

Practice asking for help: Start small. Ask for a small favor. Accept support when offered. Notice how it feels. Notice how others respond.

Practice depending on others: Let someone help you with something. Share a problem and ask for advice. Accept emotional support. These are acts of trust and connection.

Self-reliance doesn't have to be all-or-nothing. You can be independent and connected. You can handle things yourself and also accept help.

Challenging emotional suppression

Emotional suppression is another core avoidant behavior. You might dismiss feelings, intellectualize them, or simply not notice them.

Practice emotional expression: When you notice an emotion, name it. Share it with someone. Write about it. Don't dismiss it. Don't intellectualize it. Just feel it and express it.

Start with small emotions: Share a small feeling. 'I'm feeling a bit anxious about this.' 'I'm feeling grateful for that.' Build from there.

Emotional expression is vulnerable, but it's also connecting. When you share how you feel, others can understand you better. They can respond. They can connect.
