Module 3 • Teaching Reading: Building Literacy with Joy

Overview
  • Reading is one of the most important skills children learn, but it's also one that can become a source of pressure and stress. This module helps you teach reading in ways that are developmentally appropriate and joyful.
  • We explore the five components of reading: phonemic awareness, phonics, vocabulary, fluency, and comprehension. Understanding these helps you support your child's reading development holistically.
  • The module introduces pre-reading skills—the foundations that come before decoding words. These include: Print awareness, Letter recognition, Phonemic awareness, Vocabulary development, Story comprehension.
  • You'll learn how to read aloud effectively—not just reading the words, but engaging with the story, asking questions, and making connections. This builds comprehension and love of reading.
  • We discuss when and how to introduce phonics. Some children are ready early; others need more time. The key is following your child's lead and making it playful.

Key Takeaways
  • Reading develops through stages—honor each stage rather than rushing ahead.
  • Pre-reading skills are as important as decoding—build strong foundations.
  • Reading aloud is one of the most powerful ways to support literacy development.
  • Make reading playful and enjoyable—pressure creates resistance.

Reading is more than decoding

When we think about teaching reading, we often focus on phonics—teaching children to sound out words. But reading is so much more than decoding. It's understanding stories, making connections, asking questions, and finding meaning.

Children who learn to decode but don't understand what they're reading aren't really reading—they're word-calling. True reading involves comprehension, engagement, and connection.

This module helps you support all aspects of reading development: the technical skills (phonics, decoding) and the deeper skills (comprehension, engagement, love of stories).

Pre-reading foundations

Before children can read, they need pre-reading skills: Print awareness—understanding that print carries meaning, that we read left to right, that words are made of letters; Letter recognition—knowing letter names and shapes; Phonemic awareness—hearing and manipulating sounds in words; Vocabulary—knowing the meanings of words; Story comprehension—understanding narratives, characters, and plots.

These skills develop naturally through: Reading aloud daily, Playing with letters and sounds, Singing songs and rhymes, Talking about stories, Playing word games.

You don't need formal lessons for these skills—they develop through rich, language-rich experiences. Read to your child daily. Talk about stories. Play with words. These simple activities build the foundation for reading.

When to introduce phonics

Phonics instruction—teaching the relationship between letters and sounds—is important, but timing matters. Most children are ready for formal phonics instruction between ages 4-6, but readiness varies.

Signs of readiness include: Knowing most letter names, Showing interest in words and reading, Being able to hear sounds in words, Having the attention span for short lessons, Asking how to spell words or read signs.

When your child shows these signs, you can introduce phonics playfully: Through games, Through books, Through writing, Through songs, Through everyday experiences.

Keep it short, fun, and pressure-free. Five minutes of playful phonics is more effective than 30 minutes of drills. Follow your child's interest and energy.

Reading aloud: Your secret weapon

Reading aloud is one of the most powerful ways to support literacy development. When you read to your child, you're: Building vocabulary, Developing comprehension, Modeling fluent reading, Creating positive associations with books, Strengthening your relationship.

Make reading aloud a daily ritual. Choose books your child enjoys. Read with expression. Ask questions. Make connections. Let your child interrupt and ask questions. Make it interactive and engaging.

Even after your child learns to read, continue reading aloud. Read books that are above their reading level. This exposes them to rich vocabulary and complex ideas they might not access independently yet.

Homework
  • Read aloud daily for at least 15 minutes. Make it interactive and engaging. Notice how your child's comprehension and engagement develop.
  • Complete your Pre-Reading Skills Assessment. Create a plan to support areas that need development through play and everyday experiences.
  • If your child shows phonics readiness, introduce playful phonics activities: Letter-sound games, Word building, Sound hunts. Keep it fun and pressure-free.
