Module 4 • Teaching Math: Building Numeracy Through Play

Overview
  • Math anxiety often starts early, but it doesn't have to. This module helps you introduce math concepts in ways that are playful, meaningful, and developmentally appropriate.
  • We explore early math concepts: number sense, counting, patterns, shapes, measurement, and problem-solving. These develop through play and everyday experiences, not worksheets.
  • The module introduces 'math talk'—using mathematical language in everyday conversations. 'How many apples do we need?' 'Which is bigger?' 'Can you share these equally?' This builds math vocabulary and concepts naturally.
  • You'll learn about the importance of concrete experiences before abstract concepts. Children need to touch, manipulate, and explore before they can understand symbols and equations.
  • We discuss how to make math visible in your home: measuring while cooking, counting while setting the table, comparing sizes, finding patterns. Math is everywhere—you just need to notice it together.
  • Finally, we address math anxiety and how to prevent it. When math is playful and meaningful, children develop positive associations. When it's pressure-filled and abstract, anxiety develops.
Key Takeaways
  • Math develops through concrete experiences before abstract concepts.
  • Math talk in everyday life builds math vocabulary and concepts naturally.
  • Play-based math activities are more effective than worksheets for young children.
  • Making math visible and meaningful prevents math anxiety.

Math is everywhere

Math isn't just numbers on a page—it's patterns in nature, shapes in buildings, measurements in cooking, and quantities in sharing. When you help your child see math in the world around them, you're building a foundation for mathematical thinking.

Young children are natural mathematicians. They compare sizes, count objects, notice patterns, and solve problems. Your job isn't to teach them math—it's to notice the math they're already doing and help them go deeper.

This module helps you recognize mathematical thinking in everyday moments and support it through play, conversation, and exploration.

Exercise 1

Math Observation: For one week, notice math in your daily life: Counting, Measuring, Comparing, Patterns, Shapes, Problem-solving. Document what you notice.

Early math concepts

Early math includes: Number sense—understanding what numbers mean, that 5 is more than 3, that numbers can be broken apart and put together; Counting—one-to-one correspondence, cardinality (the last number counted is the total), counting on; Patterns—recognizing, creating, and extending patterns; Shapes and spatial thinking—recognizing shapes, understanding position (above, below, beside), spatial reasoning; Measurement—comparing sizes, lengths, weights, understanding units; Problem-solving—using math to solve real problems.

These concepts develop through: Playing with blocks, Cooking together, Sorting and categorizing, Playing board games, Exploring nature, Building and creating.

You don't need formal math lessons—you need to notice math in play and support it through questions and conversation.

Exercise 2

Math Talk Practice: Practice using mathematical language in everyday conversations. Set a goal: Use math talk at least 5 times per day. Notice how your child responds.

Concrete before abstract

Children need to touch, manipulate, and explore before they can understand symbols. Before they can understand '2 + 3 = 5,' they need to physically combine two objects with three objects and count the total.

Use manipulatives—objects children can touch and move. These might include: Counting objects (blocks, buttons, beans), Pattern blocks, Measuring tools (rulers, scales, cups), Shape puzzles, Number lines.

Let children explore these materials freely. Then, when they're ready, introduce mathematical language: 'You have 3 blocks. If you add 2 more, how many will you have?'

Only introduce symbols (numbers, equations) after children understand the concepts concretely. Rushing to abstract symbols before concrete understanding creates confusion and anxiety.

Exercise 3

Concrete Math Activities: Choose one math concept (counting, patterns, shapes, measurement). Create a concrete, hands-on activity. Let your child explore freely, then introduce mathematical language.

Math talk

Math talk is using mathematical language in everyday conversations. Instead of 'Put your toys away,' try 'Can you put 5 toys in the box?' Instead of 'We're leaving soon,' try 'We're leaving in 10 minutes—that's 600 seconds.'

Math talk builds: Math vocabulary, Number sense, Measurement concepts, Spatial language, Problem-solving skills.

You don't need to plan math talk—just notice opportunities and use mathematical language naturally. The more you do it, the more natural it becomes.
