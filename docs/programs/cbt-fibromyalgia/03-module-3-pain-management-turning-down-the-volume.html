Module 3 • Pain Management: Turning Down the Volume

Overview
  • This module focuses on strategies for managing fibromyalgia pain, recognizing that complete pain elimination may not be possible, but pain reduction and better coping are achievable.
  • We explore 'Pain Gate Control Theory'—how your brain can amplify or reduce pain signals based on attention, emotions, and other factors.
  • You will learn 'Distraction Techniques'—engaging your mind in activities that reduce pain awareness.
  • The concept of 'Pleasant Activity Scheduling' is introduced—engaging in enjoyable activities despite pain, which can reduce pain intensity.
  • We address 'Relaxation for Pain'—using breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, and imagery to reduce pain and muscle tension.
  • You will practice 'Cognitive Restructuring for Pain'—challenging catastrophic thoughts about pain and replacing them with balanced perspectives.
  • Finally, we create a 'Pain Management Toolkit'—a collection of strategies you can use when pain flares.
Key Takeaways
  • Pain intensity can be influenced by attention, emotions, and thoughts.
  • Distraction and pleasant activities can reduce pain awareness.
  • Relaxation techniques can reduce muscle tension and pain.
  • Catastrophic thoughts about pain often worsen the experience.

Pain is complex

Fibromyalgia pain is complex. It's not just a physical sensation—it's influenced by your attention, your emotions, your thoughts, your stress levels.

This doesn't mean the pain isn't real. It means you have more influence over it than you might think. You can't eliminate it entirely, but you can reduce its intensity. You can change your relationship with it.

We're going to learn techniques that work with your nervous system, not against it. We're going to turn down the volume on pain.

Exercise 1

The Pain-Focus Experiment: For one day, notice how pain intensity changes based on what you're doing. When is it worse? When is it better? What activities reduce awareness?

The pain gate

Your brain has a 'pain gate'—it can amplify or reduce pain signals. When you focus intensely on pain, the gate opens wider. When you're distracted or engaged, the gate closes.

This is why pain often feels worse at night when you're trying to sleep—there's nothing else to focus on. And why it might feel better during an engaging activity—your attention is elsewhere.

We can use this. We can engage in activities that close the gate. We can use distraction. We can schedule pleasant activities. We can reduce the pain's dominance in your life.

Exercise 2

Create Your Pleasant Activity List: List 20 activities you enjoy (or used to enjoy) that are still possible. Include both active and passive activities.

Working with pain, not against it

Fighting pain often makes it worse. Tension, stress, catastrophic thoughts—these amplify pain signals.

But we can work with pain. We can relax around it. We can accept its presence while reducing its impact. We can engage in life despite it.

This isn't about giving up. It's about finding a different approach. One that's more effective. One that reduces suffering, even when pain persists.

Exercise 3

The Pain Thought Record: When pain is high, write down your thoughts. Are they catastrophic? ('This will never end.') Challenge them. Find balanced alternatives.

Homework
  • Schedule 3 pleasant activities this week, even if pain is present. Notice how engagement affects pain awareness.
  • Practice relaxation techniques daily (breathing, progressive muscle relaxation). Notice impact on pain and muscle tension.
  • Use distraction techniques when pain flares: engage in an absorbing activity (puzzle, book, conversation, etc.).
