Module 3 • Understanding Trauma's Impact: Beliefs and Meaning

Overview
  • Trauma doesn't just affect your memories—it affects your beliefs about yourself, others, and the world. These beliefs, called 'stuck points,' can keep you trapped in trauma responses long after the event is over.
  • We explore common stuck points in trauma: safety ('The world is dangerous'), trust ('I can't trust anyone'), power ('I'm powerless'), esteem ('I'm damaged/broken'), and intimacy ('I don't deserve love').
  • The module helps you identify your own stuck points—beliefs that developed as a result of trauma and that now interfere with your life. These beliefs feel true, but they're interpretations, not facts.
  • You'll learn to recognize how stuck points affect your thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. Understanding this connection helps you see how changing beliefs can change your life.
  • We introduce the concept of 'assimilation' and 'accommodation'—how trauma can lead you to either blame yourself (assimilation) or see the world as entirely dangerous (accommodation). Recovery involves finding balance.
  • Finally, we begin the process of challenging stuck points, gently and compassionately. You'll learn that beliefs can change, that you can develop new, more balanced perspectives that honor your experience while allowing you to move forward.
Key Takeaways
  • Trauma affects your beliefs about yourself, others, and the world.

The beliefs trauma creates

After trauma, your beliefs about the world change. This makes sense—something terrible happened, and your brain is trying to make sense of it. But sometimes, the beliefs that develop are too extreme, too absolute, keeping you stuck in trauma responses.

These beliefs are called 'stuck points'—thoughts that feel true but that developed from trauma and now interfere with your life. Common stuck points include: 'The world is dangerous' (safety), 'I can't trust anyone' (trust), 'I'm powerless' (power), 'I'm damaged/broken' (esteem), 'I don't deserve love' (intimacy).

These beliefs feel true because they developed from real experience. But they're interpretations, not facts. The world can be dangerous AND safe. You can trust some people AND protect yourself. You can be affected by trauma AND still be whole.

Recovery involves examining these beliefs, challenging the ones that are too extreme, and developing more balanced perspectives that honor your experience while allowing you to move forward.

Five areas of belief

Trauma typically affects five areas of belief: Safety—beliefs about how safe the world is ('The world is dangerous,' 'I'm not safe anywhere'); Trust—beliefs about whether others can be trusted ('I can't trust anyone,' 'People will hurt me'); Power—beliefs about control and agency ('I'm powerless,' 'I have no control'); Esteem—beliefs about self-worth ('I'm damaged,' 'I'm broken,' 'It's my fault'); Intimacy—beliefs about relationships ('I don't deserve love,' 'I'm unlovable').

These beliefs developed as your brain tried to make sense of what happened. They served a purpose—they tried to protect you. But now, they're too extreme, keeping you stuck.

Recovery involves examining each area, identifying your stuck points, and developing more balanced beliefs. This doesn't mean denying what happened—it means finding perspectives that are accurate AND allow you to live fully.

Assimilation vs. accommodation

When trauma happens, your brain tries to make sense of it in two ways: Assimilation—fitting the trauma into your existing beliefs ('This happened because I'm bad/weak/stupid'—blaming yourself), Accommodation—changing your beliefs to fit the trauma ('The world is entirely dangerous'—seeing everything as threatening).

Both are attempts to make sense of trauma, but both can be problematic. Assimilation leads to self-blame and shame. Accommodation leads to seeing everything as dangerous and avoiding life.

Recovery involves finding balance: 'Bad things happened, AND I'm not to blame. The world can be dangerous, AND it can also be safe. I was powerless in that situation, AND I have power in other situations.'

This balanced thinking honors your experience while allowing you to move forward.

Exercise 3

Assimilation/Accommodation Check: For each stuck point, ask: 'Is this assimilation (blaming myself) or accommodation (seeing everything as dangerous)?' Notice patterns.

How beliefs affect your life

Your beliefs shape your thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. If you believe 'The world is dangerous,' you might: Think—'I need to be constantly vigilant,' Feel—anxious, hypervigilant, on edge, Behave—avoid situations, isolate, constantly scan for threats.

If you believe 'I'm damaged,' you might: Think—'I don't deserve good things,' Feel—shame, worthlessness, despair, Behave—isolate, self-sabotage, avoid opportunities.

Understanding this connection helps you see how changing beliefs can change your life. When you develop more balanced beliefs, your thoughts, feelings, and behaviors change too.
