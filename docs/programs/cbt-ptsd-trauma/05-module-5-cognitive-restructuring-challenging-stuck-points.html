Module 5 • Cognitive Restructuring: Challenging Stuck Points

Overview
  • Cognitive restructuring helps you challenge and change stuck points—beliefs that developed from trauma and now interfere with your life. This module teaches you to examine these beliefs, gather evidence, and develop more balanced perspectives.
  • We explore the Thought Record—a structured tool for examining stuck points. You'll learn to identify stuck points, examine evidence for and against them, and develop balanced alternatives.
  • The module helps you challenge common stuck points: self-blame ('It was my fault'), safety ('The world is entirely dangerous'), trust ('I can't trust anyone'), power ('I'm powerless'), and esteem ('I'm damaged').
  • You'll learn to distinguish between facts and interpretations. Stuck points often feel like facts, but they're interpretations. Learning to see this distinction is key to change.
  • We explore the concept of 'stuck point logs'—tracking stuck points as they appear and systematically challenging them. This helps you develop new, more balanced beliefs.
  • Finally, we introduce behavioral experiments—testing new beliefs in real life. These experiments provide evidence that challenges old stuck points and supports new beliefs.
Key Takeaways
  • Stuck points are interpretations, not facts—they can be challenged and changed.
  • Thought records help you examine stuck points systematically.
  • Distinguishing between facts and interpretations is key to change.
  • Behavioral experiments provide real-world evidence for new beliefs.

Challenging what feels true

Stuck points feel true. They feel like facts. 'The world is dangerous.' 'I'm damaged.' 'It was my fault.' These beliefs developed from real experience, so they carry the weight of truth.

But they're interpretations, not facts. They're one way of seeing things, not the only way. Cognitive restructuring helps you examine these beliefs, gather evidence, and develop more balanced perspectives.

This doesn't mean denying what happened or minimizing your experience. It means finding perspectives that are accurate AND allow you to move forward. 'Bad things happened, AND I'm not to blame.' 'The world can be dangerous, AND it can also be safe.'

Challenging stuck points takes courage. It means questioning beliefs that feel true, that have protected you. But it's also liberating. When you challenge stuck points, you're taking back your power. You're saying: 'I get to decide what I believe.'

Exercise 1

Stuck Point Log: Keep a log of stuck points as they appear. For each, complete a thought record: Identify the stuck point, Notice when it appears, Examine evidence for and against, Develop a balanced belief, Notice how this affects your feelings and behaviors.

The thought record

A thought record is a structured tool for examining stuck points. It helps you: Identify the stuck point, Notice when it appears, Examine evidence for and against it, Develop a more balanced belief, Notice how changing the belief changes your feelings and behaviors.

For example, if your stuck point is 'It was my fault,' you might examine: Evidence for—'I was there,' 'I didn't stop it'; Evidence against—'I was a child,' 'I was overpowered,' 'The responsibility was the perpetrator's'; Balanced belief—'I was there, AND it wasn't my fault. The responsibility belongs to the person who harmed me.'

This process takes time. Be patient. Be compassionate. Changing beliefs that developed from trauma is hard work.

Exercise 2

Common Stuck Point Challenge: Choose one common stuck point (self-blame, safety, trust, power, or esteem). Complete a detailed thought record. Challenge it systematically. Develop a balanced belief.

Common stuck points and challenges

Common stuck points include: Self-blame—'It was my fault.' Challenge: 'What would I tell a friend in this situation? What's the evidence?'; Safety—'The world is entirely dangerous.' Challenge: 'Are there safe places? Safe people? Can danger and safety coexist?'; Trust—'I can't trust anyone.' Challenge: 'Are there people I can trust? Can I trust selectively?'; Power—'I'm powerless.' Challenge: 'What power do I have now? What choices can I make?'; Esteem—'I'm damaged.' Challenge: 'Am I entirely damaged, or am I affected by trauma AND still whole?'

For each stuck point, gather evidence. Challenge extreme thinking. Develop balanced perspectives. This takes practice, but it gets easier over time.

Exercise 3

Facts vs. Interpretations Exercise: For a recent situation, identify: Facts—what actually happened, Interpretations—what meanings you assigned. Practice distinguishing between them.

Facts vs. interpretations

Learning to distinguish between facts and interpretations is crucial. Facts are observable, verifiable. Interpretations are meanings we assign to facts.

For example: Fact—'Someone hurt me.' Interpretation—'I'm damaged,' 'It was my fault,' 'I don't deserve love.'

The fact is true. The interpretations might not be. You can acknowledge the fact while challenging interpretations that don't serve you.

Exercise 4

Behavioral Experiment: Choose one stuck point. Design an experiment to test a new, balanced belief. For example, if your stuck point is 'I can't trust anyone,' experiment with trusting a safe person in a small way. Document results.

Homework
  • Maintain your Stuck Point Log for two weeks. Complete thought records for stuck points as they appear. Notice patterns.
  • Choose one major stuck point to work with. Complete a detailed thought record. Challenge it systematically. Develop and practice a balanced belief.
  • Practice distinguishing facts from interpretations daily. When you notice interpretations, ask: 'Is this a fact or an interpretation? What's the evidence?'
  • Conduct at least one behavioral experiment per week. Test new beliefs in real life. Document results and notice what you learn.
