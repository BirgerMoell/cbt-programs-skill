Module 2 • Pacing: Breaking the Boom-Bust Cycle

Overview
  • This module addresses the most common behavioral trap in chronic pain: The Boom-Bust Cycle. Overdoing it on 'good days' and crashing on 'bad days.'
  • We introduce 'Time-Contingent' vs. 'Pain-Contingent' activity. You will learn to stop based on a plan, not based on pain.
  • You will calculate your 'Baselines' for common activities (sitting, standing, walking). Finding the safe amount you can do without flaring.
  • The concept of 'The Sweet Spot' is explained—finding the middle ground between inactivity (rust) and overactivity (bust).
  • We create a Pacing Plan. Breaking large tasks into small, manageable chunks with mandatory rest breaks.
  • We address the guilt of resting. You will learn that rest is a strategic tool for productivity, not laziness.
  • Finally, we practice 'Graded Activity.' Slowly increasing your baselines over time to retrain the nervous system's tolerance.
Key Takeaways
  • Pushing through pain on good days leads to long recovery periods (the Bust).
  • Pacing means stopping *before* the pain spikes, not after.
  • Rest breaks should be scheduled, not just taken when you collapse.
  • Consistency is more important than intensity.

The weekend warrior trap

You wake up feeling decent. 'Finally!' you think. 'I can catch up on chores.' You clean the house, mow the lawn, and go shopping. You ignore the twinges. By evening, you are in agony. You spend the next three days in bed recovering.

This is the Boom-Bust cycle. It feels productive, but it actually keeps your pain system sensitive. It teaches your body that activity equals danger.

We want to smooth out the jagged line of your activity. We want to do a moderate amount every day—even on bad days, and (crucially) *only* a moderate amount on good days.

Exercise 1

Find Your Baselines: Measure how long you can do an activity (e.g., washing dishes) before pain flares. Do this for 3 days. Your baseline is roughly 80% of the average.

Time-contingent pacing

Most people stop an activity when the pain gets bad. That is 'Pain-Contingent.' It lets the pain be the boss.

We are going to switch to 'Time-Contingent.' You decide in advance: 'I will walk for 10 minutes.' If you feel good at 10 minutes, you stop anyway. If you feel bad, you (gently) try to reach 10 minutes.

By stopping while you still feel okay, you avoid the flare-up. You teach your brain that activity is safe. Over time, we increase the time. 10 minutes becomes 11, then 12. Slowly, you regain your life.

Exercise 2

Create a Rest Schedule: Plan a task (e.g., gardening). Schedule a 5-minute break every 15 minutes. Set a timer. Obey the timer.

The tortoise wins

Pacing is frustrating. It requires holding back when you want to rush. It requires moving when you want to freeze. But it works.

Think of the tortoise and the hare. The hare runs fast and crashes. The tortoise keeps moving, slow and steady. In chronic pain, the tortoise always wins.

Exercise 3

The 10% Rule: Plan to increase one activity by just 10% next week. (e.g., walk 11 minutes instead of 10).

Homework
  • Implement time-contingent pacing for one major activity (e.g., walking or computer work) all week.
  • Catch yourself in a 'Boom' mindset (doing too much) and deliberately stop early.
  • Practice 'Active Rest'—during breaks, breathe or stretch, don't just scroll on your phone.
