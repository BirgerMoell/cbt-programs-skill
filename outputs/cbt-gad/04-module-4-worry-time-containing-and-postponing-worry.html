Module 4 • Worry Time: Containing and Postponing Worry

Overview
  • Worry Time is a powerful technique for containing worry. Instead of trying to stop worrying (which usually makes it worse), you schedule a specific time each day to worry. Outside of this time, when worries appear, you postpone them until your scheduled worry time.
  • This technique works because it breaks the cycle of constant worry. Instead of worrying all day, you contain worry to a specific time. This reduces the amount of time spent worrying and helps you see that worries can wait.
  • We'll guide you through setting up your worry time: choosing a time and place, creating a worry list, and learning to postpone worries that appear outside of this time.
  • The module teaches you to distinguish between worries that need attention (problem-solving) and worries that are just rumination. During worry time, you focus on problem-solving; rumination gets postponed or dismissed.
  • You'll learn techniques for postponing worry effectively: writing worries down, setting a reminder, and redirecting your attention. These techniques help you break the habit of immediate worry.
  • Finally, we explore how worry time helps you see that most worries don't need immediate attention. Many worries that feel urgent in the moment seem less important when you return to them later.
Key Takeaways
  • Worry Time contains worry to a specific time, reducing constant worry throughout the day.
  • Postponing worry breaks the habit of immediate worry and shows that worries can wait.
  • Distinguishing between problem-solving and rumination helps you use worry time effectively.
  • Most worries feel less urgent when postponed and revisited later.

Containing the worry

Have you ever tried to stop worrying? If so, you know it doesn't work. The more you try to suppress worry, the more it persists. It's like trying not to think about a pink elephant—the instruction itself makes you think about it.

Worry Time takes a different approach. Instead of trying to stop worrying, you contain it. You schedule a specific time each day to worry—say, 6:00 PM for 15 minutes. Outside of this time, when worries appear, you write them down and postpone them until your scheduled worry time.

This might sound strange. Why would you schedule time to worry? But here's what happens: When you know you have a designated time to worry, you don't need to worry right now. You can postpone it. This breaks the cycle of constant worry.

Over time, you'll notice something interesting: Many worries that felt urgent in the moment seem less important when you return to them during worry time. Some worries disappear entirely. Others turn out to be problems you can actually solve. Very few require the constant attention worry suggests.

Exercise 1

Worry Time Setup: Choose a specific time and place for your daily worry time. Set it up: Choose time (not too close to bedtime), Choose place, Get a notebook or app for your worry list, Set a reminder on your phone.

Setting up your worry time

Setting up worry time involves: Choosing a specific time each day (not too close to bedtime, as worry can interfere with sleep), Choosing a specific place (a chair, a desk, somewhere you can focus), Setting a timer (start with 15-20 minutes), Creating a worry list (a notebook or app where you write worries to postpone), Committing to use this time consistently.

During worry time, you: Review your worry list, Focus on problem-solving for worries that have solutions, Postpone or dismiss worries that are just rumination, Set a timer and stick to it—when time is up, worry time is over.

Outside of worry time, when worries appear, you: Acknowledge the worry ('I notice I'm worrying about X'), Write it down on your worry list, Remind yourself: 'I'll worry about this during my worry time at 6 PM', Redirect your attention to the present moment.

This takes practice. At first, worries will keep appearing. That's normal. Gently redirect your attention each time. Over time, your brain learns: 'Worries can wait. I have a time for them.'

Exercise 2

Worry List Practice: For one week, whenever a worry appears outside of worry time, write it down on your worry list. Don't worry about it—just write it down. During worry time, review the list and focus on problem-solving.

Problem-solving vs. rumination

Not all worry is the same. Some worry is problem-solving—it identifies a real problem and considers solutions. Other worry is rumination—it goes over the same concerns repeatedly without leading to action.

During worry time, focus on problem-solving: Identify the problem, Brainstorm solutions, Choose a solution, Make a plan, Take action (if action is needed). If a worry doesn't have a solution or action step, it's likely rumination—and rumination can be postponed or dismissed.

Learning to distinguish between these helps you use worry time effectively. Problem-solving is useful; rumination is not. Worry time is for problem-solving, not rumination.

Exercise 3

Postponement Practice: When worries appear outside of worry time, practice postponing them: Acknowledge the worry, Write it down, Remind yourself of your worry time, Redirect attention. Do this for one week, noticing how it feels.

The art of postponing worry

Postponing worry is a skill. When a worry appears outside of worry time, you: Acknowledge it without judgment ('I notice I'm worrying'), Write it down ('I'll add this to my worry list'), Set a mental reminder ('I'll worry about this at 6 PM'), Redirect your attention ('Right now, I'm focusing on [current activity]').

This doesn't mean suppressing the worry or pretending it doesn't exist. It means acknowledging it and choosing when to address it. You're not ignoring the worry—you're scheduling it.

At first, worries will resist postponement. They'll feel urgent. They'll demand attention. That's normal. Gently redirect your attention each time. Remind yourself: 'This can wait. I have a time for it.' Over time, your brain learns that worries can wait, and they become less urgent.

Exercise 4

Problem-Solving During Worry Time: During your scheduled worry time, review your worry list. For each worry, ask: 'Is this problem-solving or rumination? If it's problem-solving, what action can I take? If it's rumination, can I postpone or dismiss it?'

Homework
  • Set up and maintain your Worry Time for two weeks. Use it consistently, even if you don't feel like worrying during that time.
  • Practice postponing worries outside of worry time. Write them down, set a reminder, and redirect your attention. Notice how this affects your daily experience of worry.
  • During worry time, focus on problem-solving. For worries that are just rumination, practice dismissing them or postponing them to the next day's worry time.
