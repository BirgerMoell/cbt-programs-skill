Testing your worries

Throughout this program, you've learned to challenge worried thoughts cognitively. Now it's time to test them behaviorally. Behavioral experiments are real-world tests of your worried thoughts. They help you gather evidence: Do your worries come true? Are avoided situations actually dangerous? Can you handle uncertainty?

For example, if you worry that people will judge you if you make a mistake, you might conduct an experiment: Make a small mistake intentionally (like spilling something), and observe people's reactions. Usually, you'll find that people don't judge you as harshly as worry suggests—or they don't notice at all.

Behavioral experiments are powerful because they provide direct evidence. You're not just thinking differently—you're experiencing differently. This creates new learning that challenges old patterns.

Exercise 1

Behavioral Experiment Design: Choose one worried thought. Design an experiment to test it: What's the prediction? What will you do? What will you observe? What's the expected outcome? Conduct the experiment and document results.

Facing your fears

Exposure therapy involves gradually facing situations you've been avoiding due to worry. This might include: Situations that trigger worry, Situations you avoid because of uncertainty, Situations where you seek excessive reassurance.

Exposure works because it helps your brain learn that these situations are safe. Every time you face a feared situation without the catastrophic outcome worry predicted, your brain learns: 'This situation isn't as dangerous as I thought.'

Start with situations that cause mild to moderate anxiety. As you build confidence, gradually increase the difficulty. The key is to stay in the situation long enough for your anxiety to decrease naturally—this is called 'habituation.'

Exposure isn't about throwing yourself into terrifying situations. It's about gradual, systematic facing of fears, with support and at your own pace.

Exercise 2

Exposure Hierarchy: Create a list of avoided situations, ranked from least to most anxiety-provoking. Start with the easiest and work your way up, practicing each until anxiety decreases.

Approach vs. avoidance

When you have GAD, avoidance feels like the solution. If something makes you anxious, why not avoid it? But avoidance maintains anxiety. Every time you avoid something because of worry, you're teaching your brain: 'This is too dangerous to face.'

Approach behaviors do the opposite. They teach your brain: 'I can face this. I can handle it. It's not as dangerous as I thought.'

Approach behaviors include: Doing things despite worry, Facing situations you've been avoiding, Making decisions without certainty, Tolerating uncertainty without reassurance-seeking.

Each approach behavior is a vote for recovery. Each one teaches your brain that you're more capable than worry suggests.

Exercise 3

Approach Behavior Practice: Choose one thing you've been avoiding due to worry. Make a plan to approach it. Do it. Notice what happens. Document the experience.

Your maintenance plan

Recovery from GAD is a journey, not a destination. You'll have good days and difficult days. You'll have periods of progress and periods of challenge. This is normal—not a sign of failure.

A maintenance plan helps you stay on track. It includes: Daily practices (mindfulness, worry time, thought records), Weekly practices (behavioral experiments, exposure exercises), Warning signs (what indicates worry is increasing), Action plan (what to do when warning signs appear), Supports (people, resources, techniques that help), Values (what matters to you, what you're working toward).

Think of your maintenance plan as a roadmap. When you feel lost, you can refer to it. When you encounter obstacles, you can adjust it. It's a living document that evolves with you.

Exercise 4

Navigating setbacks

Setbacks are normal. You might have a difficult week, a period of increased worry, or a return of old patterns. This doesn't mean you've failed. It means you're human, and recovery isn't linear.

When setbacks occur: Acknowledge them without judgment ('I'm having a difficult time right now'), Review your maintenance plan ('What tools can I use?'), Practice self-compassion ('This is hard, and that's okay'), Take small steps ('What's one thing I can do today?'), Reach out for support ('Who can I talk to?').

Setbacks are opportunities to practice what you've learned. They're not failures—they're part of the journey. How you respond to setbacks matters more than the setbacks themselves.

Celebrating your progress

Take a moment to acknowledge how far you've come. You've learned powerful tools: Cognitive restructuring, Worry time, Uncertainty tolerance, Mindfulness, Behavioral experiments, Exposure. You've practiced them. You've grown.

Recovery from GAD is possible. You've seen evidence of this. You've experienced moments of freedom from worry, moments of present-moment awareness, moments of tolerance for uncertainty. These moments will grow. They'll become more frequent. They'll become your new normal.

This program has given you the tools. Now it's about using them consistently, compassionately, and with patience. Your future self is counting on you. You can do this.

  • Complete your Exposure Hierarchy and begin practicing exposure exercises. Start with the easiest situations and gradually work your way up. Practice each until anxiety decreases.
